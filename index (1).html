Elfin Cove forum threads & lodge reports

2026 rules that will surprise you

Current emergency orders — these are real, this year
  • Yelloweye & friends (demersal shelf rockfish), nonresidents: season is Jul 1–Aug 25, one per day — and ONE for the entire year, recorded on your license the moment it hits the deck. Make your one count.
  • Pelagic rockfish (black bass etc.): 3 per day, 6 in possession. Released rockfish must go back down via descending device — required gear on every boat.
  • No filleting at sea of lingcod, kings, coho, or nonpelagic rockfish through Sep 13 — fish come back to the dock whole for creel sampling.
  • Halibut is federally managed — charter (guided) rules differ from unguided. Your captain lives these rules; when in doubt, ask before the fish goes in the box.
  • King annual limit just went UP: effective July 1, nonresidents get two kings for the year (was one) — ADF&G raised it June 25 because harvest was running behind. Details in the rulebook below.

Fish ID — know it before you keep it

The mouth and the tail tell you everything
The 60-second salmon check: open the mouth first. Black gums = king (write it down!). White gum line = coho. Then the tail: huge oval blotches on both lobes = pink · a few spots, upper lobe only = coho · spots both lobes + black gums = king · no spots at all = chum. On the bottom: lateral line arches over the fin = halibut; toothy grin and a skinny body = arrowtooth, re-bait. Any rockfish that isn’t plain gray-black: assume it’s in the one-a-year club until proven otherwise.

King (Chinook) salmon

KEEP · 1/DAY · 28″+
  • Open the mouth: black gums, black mouth — that’s the whole test.
  • Spots on the back AND on both lobes of the tail.
  • Thick-bodied; anything pushing 15 lb+ is almost certainly a king.

Coho (silver) salmon

KEEP · 6/DAY
  • Black mouth but the gum line is white — check where teeth meet jaw.
  • Spots on the upper tail lobe only.
  • Chrome-bright and frantic; jumps far more than a king.

Pink salmon

KEEP · 6/DAY
  • Huge oval spots plastered on both tail lobes — biggest spots of any salmon.
  • Tiny scales, small fish (3–5 lb). Males grow the hump in rivers, not out here.
  • If it’s small, silver, and the tail looks ink-blotted: pink.

Chum (dog) salmon

KEEP · 6/DAY
  • No spots anywhere — cleanest back and tail of the bunch.
  • Faint vertical calico bars show as they ripen; white edge on the anal fin.
  • Bigger eye pupil than coho; silver streaks in the tail fin rays.

Pacific halibut

KEEP · 2/DAY · ANY SIZE
  • Diamond-shaped flatfish, both eyes on the dark right side, pure white underneath.
  • The lateral line makes a clear arch over the pectoral fin — arrowtooth’s doesn’t.
  • Tail edge slightly concave; flesh firm and pearly.

Arrowtooth flounder

NOT THE ONE
  • The great halibut impostor of every gravel flat. Big mouth full of arrow-point teeth.
  • Thinner body, more deeply forked tail, grayer topside.
  • Legal, but the flesh turns mushy when cooked — release it and re-bait.

Black rockfish (pelagic)

KEEP · 3/DAY
  • Bass-shaped, dark gray-to-black, pale belly, faint pale spotting along the back.
  • Rounded anal fin and no heavy head spines = pelagic = in your 3/day bag.
  • Dusky rockfish looks nearly identical — also pelagic, also legal. Schools mid-water over kelp.

Yelloweye rockfish

ONE PER YEAR
  • Unmistakable: orange-red body, brilliant yellow eye, raspy ridges above it.
  • Adults carry a pale stripe down the lateral line.
  • Comes up from deep — decide fast: your one-a-year, or straight onto the descender.

Quillback rockfish

COUNTS AS YOUR ONE
  • Tall, deeply-notched spiny dorsal — the “quills.”
  • Brown mottle with orange-gold freckles on the head and chest.
  • Shelf/demersal group: shares the same one-a-year tally as yelloweye. When in doubt, send it down.

Lingcod

RELEASE — CLOSED
  • Long tapered body, one continuous dorsal fin, copper-brown blotch camo.
  • Huge mouth with visible canines — nothing else out there looks like it.
  • Nonresident season is closed your week: pliers over the rail, photo in the water, gone.

Halibut — the whole program

Your money technique · fish the slacks
  1. Where: flat gravel/sand in 80–250 ft — Three Hill's beach, Idaho Inlet mouth, Port Althorp outside. Halibut sit on featureless flats near where current delivers scent, not in the rocks.
  2. When: the hour around slack. Mid-tide your 2 lb of lead blows down-current and you're fishing badly. Anchor 15–20 min before the countdown above hits zero.
  3. Rig: 80–100 lb braid → sliding-sinker or spreader bar → 2–3 ft of 150 lb mono → 16/0 circle hook. Bait: half a herring PLUS a strip of octopus or salmon belly (octopus stays on; herring makes the scent).
  4. Anchor beats drifting at slack — your scent plume builds a trail that pulls fish to you. Drift only when current is moving and slow (under ~0.5 kt).
  5. Fish it: free-spool to bottom, crank up two turns. Rod in holder or dead-still. Re-drop to tap bottom every few minutes so the scent keeps pumping.
  6. The bite: tap… tap… chewing. Do NOTHING. When the rod folds over and stays folded, reel smooth and steady — the circle hook corners itself. A hookset swing pulls it straight out of a halibut's mouth.
  7. The fight: constant tension, short pumps, no slack ever. They make elevator dives at the boat — let the drag give.
The tellThat first fold — rod tip buried like someone hung a truck off it. Count "one-two" and start reeling. Never swing.

Landing a halibut without a deckhand

This is where self-guided crews get hurt — plan it
  1. Decide before it surfaces. Once it's aboard and not immediately released, it's in your bag — no trading up later.
  2. Under ~30 lb ("chicken"): net or gaff head-first, straight into the fish box. Easy.
  3. 30–80 lb: tire it until it lies flat on its side at the surface — if it's still upright and kicking, it's green. Gaff in the head, swing aboard in one motion, cut the gills immediately to bleed.
  4. Big fish: a green 100 lb halibut in a small boat breaks ankles and rods. Rope through the gills and out the mouth, cleat it off, bleed it, and tow or trail it until it's dead-still before it ever comes over the rail.
  5. Everyone else clears the deck — one person on the rod, one on the gaff, nobody's leg inside the rope's bight. Ever.
  6. Releasing? Leave it in the water, pliers on the hook or cut the leader close. Careful-release is the law for anything you don't box.
The ruleFlat and quiet at the surface = ready. Upright and kicking = give it two more minutes. There is no fish worth a gaff swing at a green one.

Kings — trolling & mooching

40–120 ft, near bait, slow and patient
  1. Troll 2.5–3.2 kt: 11″ flasher, then 40–48″ of leader to a hoochie or spoon; whole/cut-plug herring wants a slower 1.5–2.5 kt with a gentle roll — check the roll boatside before sending it down.
  2. Downrigger or 8–16 oz of lead to 40–120 ft; start deep at first light, follow the bait on the sounder.
  3. The take is soft — rubbery pull-pull, nothing like a coho slam. Rod loads: reel until it's heavy, then lift. No violent swings.
  4. First run is the whole show — thumb OFF the spool, let the drag sing. Kings dive under the boat; follow with the rod tip, in the water if you must.
  5. Net head-first, one scoop. Under 28″ stays wet and swims off. A keeper gets written on the license before lines go back down.
The tellBait "gets heavy" and the rod pulls down in slow throbs. Feed it one second, tighten, lift.

Coho — run and gun

Faster, shallower, angrier than kings
  1. Troll faster — 3.5–4.5 kt — and shallower: 15–60 ft, often the top 30. Small spoons, hoochies, or bucktails way back on flatlines.
  2. Find birds diving or bait dimpling and stay on it; silvers travel in schools, so doubles and triples happen — leave other lines in when one goes off.
  3. Rod slams flat: grab, reel FAST until tight. Coho throw hooks on any slack — keep the rod bent through every jump.
  4. No downriggers needed most days. If trolling dies, cast a spoon past the bait ball, count it down 5–10, retrieve in quick pulses.
  5. Boatside they go berserk — keep its head in the water and net in one motion.
The tellTick-tick-tick becomes a flat-out slam with line peeling. Just keep the handle turning.

Pinks & chums — light-tackle riot

Bring a spinning rod, thank yourself later
  1. Find jumpers — pinks show themselves constantly in the top 30 ft near beaches and points.
  2. Anything pink: Buzz Bomb, small Pixee spoon, 1/4–3/8 oz jig. Cast past the school, let it flutter, twitch-and-drop retrieve. Most hits come on the drop.
  3. Chums run bigger and pull harder than their reputation — they'll bend a light rod double. Same lures, a touch deeper.
  4. Soft mouths: sweep-set, steady pressure, no jerking.
  5. Perfect kid-and-spouse program while the "serious" rods soak for halibut at anchor.
The tellThe flutter stops or the line ticks sideways. Sweep and it's on.

Rockfish — and the release drill

Guaranteed dinner · strict on the way back down
  1. Kelp edges and rock in 40–150 ft: 2–6 oz jig, swimbait, or shrimp-fly dropper. Drop to bottom, crank up two turns, bounce.
  2. Black bass often eat it on the drop — if the fall stops early, reel. Soft mouths: steady, no hard sets.
  3. Know your fish: black/dusky (pelagic, 3/day) vs quillback/yelloweye (shelf — one yelloweye a YEAR). If you can't ID it, it goes back down.
  4. Descending drill: clip the device to the lower jaw, drop it to capture depth (or 100 ft) on a weighted line, pop it free. Barotrauma'd fish tossed overboard float and die — the device is why the fishery stays open.
  5. Rig the descender on its own rod BEFORE you leave the dock, so a release takes 30 seconds, not a scramble.
The tellThump-thump on the fall. Everything about rockfish is fun and easy — the discipline is the release.

Lingcod — catch, admire, return

Release-only your week — here's the clean way
  1. They'll find you: a ling grabbing a hooked rockfish feels like your fish suddenly tripled in weight. Keep slow, steady pressure — no pumping — and it usually rides all the way up jaw-clamped.
  2. Hooked one on a jig? Reel it away from the rocks in the first ten seconds or it wraps you.
  3. Boatside: leave it IN the water. Long pliers on the hook, or grip the lower jaw with a glove if it's calm. The photo happens over the rail, not on your lap.
  4. The hitchhikers that never bit anything usually let go at the surface on their own — one boatside headshake and gone.
The tellYour rockfish stops coming and the rod loads triple. That's not a snag — that's a ling with dinner. Slow and smooth.
